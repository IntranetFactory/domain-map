## Market-shape verdicts

### D1 ,  Runbook + workload automation split
**Verdict: split one out ,  promote Job Scheduling and Workload Automation to its own domain (SOAP-aligned); keep Runbook Automation inside ITOM.** Evidence: Gartner treats workload automation as the standalone **Service Orchestration and Automation Platforms (SOAP)** Magic Quadrant, whose Leaders (BMC Control-M, Stonebranch UAC, Redwood RunMyJobs, plus Broadcom AutoSys, Tidal) are sold as dedicated products with their own license, console, and scheduler engine ,  none is a feature of an ITOM monitoring suite. Their schemas master `job_definitions` as genuine first-class objects: Control-M has Job/Folder workflow objects via its Automation API, and Broadcom AutoSys defines jobs in JIL (Job Information Language) with `job_type`, machine, calendar, and file-watcher trigger attributes. So if WLA stays, `job_definitions`/`job_runs`/`job_schedules` are real masters, but the market evidence is that WLA is a distinct buy. **Runbook / IT-process automation is a separate, smaller market** (PagerDuty Runbook Automation née Rundeck, Resolve Actions, Stonebranch also straddles it) that enterprise ITOM suites *do* bundle: ServiceNow and BMC Helix both ship runbook/automation policies natively as remediation attached to events. So runbook automation belongs in ITOM (giving module 269 a real master in `runbook_definitions` + `runbook_executions` + `automation_policies`), while workload automation, which no ITOM suite natively masters, is promoted out. This makes module 269 ITOM-internal runbook/process automation with its own masters, and WLA becomes a new SOAP domain.

### D2 ,  RMM vs ITOM monitoring ownership
**Verdict: (a) ITOM canonical + RMM keeps an embedded copy (do NOT make RMM a pure consumer, do NOT fully split).** Evidence: RMM products model alerts and policies in a materially different *shape* than enterprise ITOM. In NinjaOne, ConnectWise RMM, Datto RMM, and Kaseya VSA, a "policy" is a **device-agent configuration bundle** (monitoring thresholds like CPU>85%, RAM>90%, disk<10%, SMART failures, plus patching, AV, and backup settings) pushed to an endpoint agent; an "alert" is a per-device threshold breach (ConnectWise ships 1100+ out-of-box alert conditions in a "look for everything" model). Enterprise ITOM (ServiceNow `em_event`->`em_alert`, BMC Helix events) instead correlates multi-source events to **service/CI impact** via the CMDB. The nouns are the same (`monitoring_alerts`, `monitoring_policies`) but RMM's are device-agent-scoped and ITOM's are service-impact-scoped. Because the conceptual record is the same and ITOM carries the richer correlation model, ITOM stays canonical and RMM retains its embedded copy (the existing RMM legacy-master arrangement on 85/86) rather than re-anchoring; a hard split into RMM-specific shapes is unjustified since the divergence is in field semantics and scope, not in the entity identity. Revisit only if an RMM audit shows the device-agent policy needs structurally different children (agent bindings, patch settings) that pollute the ITOM master.

### D3 ,  Observability alert ownership
**Verdict: leave as-is ,  OBS publishes a threshold-breach trigger INTO ITOM's `monitoring_alerts`; do NOT add a separate ITOM-owned observability-alert entity.** Evidence: observability platforms unambiguously master their own alert objects ,  Datadog calls them **Monitors** (the alert definition) plus a separate **Event Management** surface; Dynatrace masters **Problems** (root-cause-correlated) plus anomaly-detection/alerting-profile objects; Grafana masters **alert rules**, and Prometheus masters **Alertmanager** rules. But those alert masters live in the **OBS domain**, not ITOM. The handoff payload that lands in ITOM is the downstream operational alert/event, which is exactly `monitoring_alerts`. So the correct shape is: OBS owns its monitor/alert-rule definition entity (in the OBS domain), and the published threshold-breach instance flows into ITOM's `monitoring_alerts` master. No new entity is needed on the ITOM side; the only action item (out of scope here) is ensuring the OBS domain itself masters its monitor/alert-rule definition. ITOM stays the consumer of the OBS-emitted alert instance.

### D4 ,  AIOPS suppression ownership
**Verdict: re-anchor the "suppression applied" trigger to the AIOPS suppression entity (`alert_suppression_rules`, 722), not `monitoring_events`.** Evidence: suppression/maintenance/flapping rules are demonstrably mastered by AIOps platforms as their own objects. BigPanda ships **maintenance plans** ("conditions to suppress and silence alerts during scheduled system maintenance," with an "in maintenance" field used in correlation definitions) and consolidates flapping series into one alert; Moogsoft ships **maintenance window** fields and workflow-engine suppression; ServiceNow Predictive AIOps and Dynatrace Davis both carry maintenance-window suppression. Suppression acts on the alert/event stream but the **rule** is an AIOps-owned configuration object, which is precisely what `alert_suppression_rules` (722, AIOPS id 6) already is. A "suppression applied" event is the *application* of that AIOPS-owned rule, so the trigger should anchor to the AIOPS suppression entity rather than dangling off ITOM `monitoring_events`. Leaving it on `monitoring_events` mis-attributes ownership; the AIOPS audit will already own the rule object, so re-anchor now rather than defer.

### D5 ,  event_correlation_rules vs correlation_rules
**Verdict: distinct ,  ITOM owns `event_correlation_rules` (deterministic, author-time), AIOPS owns the ML `correlation_rules`/`event_correlations` object.** Evidence: these are genuinely two objects in the vendor surface. ServiceNow Event Management ships **deterministic, author-time correlation rules** (rule-based alert grouping evaluated on `em_event`->`em_alert`, including conditional-probability and CMDB-topology grouping) that an admin writes; BMC Helix ships analogous deterministic **event policies**. Separately, ServiceNow **Predictive AIOps** ships **ML-learned** alert grouping (anomaly-shape, business-context, textual-attribute scoring; time-based arrival-sequence patterns) that "adapts to real-life production environments to detect situations operations teams never thought of" ,  explicitly contrasted with pre-defined rules. Moogsoft and BigPanda are pure-play witnesses that ML correlation (entropy/unsupervised clustering, topological + temporal + contextual similarity) is its own object distinct from hand-authored rules. So the deterministic, human-authored `event_correlation_rules` is an ITOM Event Management master (module 267), and the ML-learned correlation is the existing AIOPS `event_correlations` (93) / `correlation_rules` concept. They are two distinct objects, not two names for one; keep them separate with ITOM owning the deterministic rule and AIOPS owning the ML correlation. (`correlation_rules` does not yet exist anywhere; the ML side is currently carried by AIOPS `event_correlations` 93.)

## Modularization hypothesis

If adopted: **Module 267 (Infrastructure Monitoring and Event Management)** gains real depth ,  it keeps `monitoring_events`/`monitoring_alerts`/`monitoring_policies` and adds deterministic `event_correlation_rules` plus `maintenance_windows` and CI/service-impact health, making it the canonical alert master that OBS publishes into and RMM embeds from. **Module 268 (Capacity Planning)** stays as-is with `capacity_records` (+ forecasts/utilization). **Module 269 (Operations Automation)** is rescoped to ITOM-internal **runbook/process automation** and finally gets its own masters (`runbook_definitions`, `runbook_executions`, `automation_policies`), resolving its current zero-master defect ,  while **Job Scheduling and Workload Automation is promoted out of ITOM into a new SOAP-aligned domain** (`job_definitions`/`job_runs`/`job_schedules`/`job_dependencies`). Suppression (722) and ML correlation (93) remain AIOPS-owned, with ITOM's two AIOPS-facing triggers re-anchored to those AIOPS entities.
